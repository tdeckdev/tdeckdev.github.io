Damm, here we are again, almost a full year after the last post. I most say, I'm not surprised I didn't fully commit at the time, I had a wave of positive energy that came out as fast as it came. And to make matters worse, life had to stick it in somehow. Won't go into details as to be as anonymous as possible, but a lot of personal hardships in all of my life areas were a given, medical, personal, emotial, professional, you know what I mean. Not a good set of circumstances to be completely honest. And to top it all off, the biggest tech revolution of the century didn't also help. So with a not-so-great segway, I think we can talk about one of the main focal points of this blog.

## AI, and what it means for someone who actually loves to code

So, AI was already starting to gain traction back when I started (or attempted to start) this blog last year. Since then, AI in the world of software development has done nothing but just keep rolling and has pretty much cemented itself as a core aspect of the trade. AI won't go anywhere, so that means that we developers need to be on top of things to stay employed and make a living, and so far I haven't had issue with that actually. I won't say I'm an AI savant, where I have "20 agents running" at work while like people at social media claim they do, but I know my way arround as well as the average developer, I have AI read my Jira, do the code, get corrected, apply tests, make the PR, etc, etc. But the fact of the matter is, while I think most developers have understood this, there is a subset of us that just feel at unease, and I don't think that will ever change.

What I have realized, is that the things AI replaces are also the things I like to do as a developer. With AI, we can explain our issue and watch before our eyes as it takes care of that faster than us and with code we probably would have done ourselves. And as the tools gets perfected, the imperfections on that code reduce in size. This has made developers into glorified code editors. We make the request, we see the result, and we ask for revisions until the result is what we were expecting. And while some would probably be satisfied with being sidelined to this role, I have realized I don't feel that way. Truth is, before AI I don't think I never even really thought about why I choose to be a software developer, but I do now. 

I love the act of "reasoning code" (if that can ever be a term).

I realized, that the process of looking at a software problem, thinking hard about solutions, doing something, failing, trying again and again, researching ideas, try something you didn't know, failing again, trying again, failing, trying, failing and finally seeing my work is a process I won't ever have the pleasure of doing on a professional setting again (And if you are going to say you can do this with AI, I don't think I need to directly explain what I mean by this). That seriously bummed me out at first. And what made the shock even greater is the fact of how the greater majority in the online discourse doesn't feel the same way I do. If you go online, you only see discussion about the newest model being out, the new way to manage agents, how another famous tool is integrating AI, hell, now complete projects, games and software are being decomped/reversed engineered, and AI clones get done in a matter of days. And alongside all that, is myself, just thinking, what happened to the love of the process? The pride on coming up with the best solution, something that other in your space would look at with admiration, a piece of software that is built with care and intention. And is not like AI makes "bad" code now, but the process is completely different, not even remotely what you would call software development. Figures I looked up to now just talk about "how to make AI do this better for you", and is certainly disheartening. I also realized that most developers never quite liked this aspect of software as I did, I think they just like the feeling of "making something", just having an app or website that does something, without effort, no idea how it works on the background or why is it "yours".

I also don't mean by this that I am a good developer. Hell I think I am awful at moments, but the fact is that I kept learning, kept improving, made new approaches to problems, researched how to best do something. This process is now hindered by, or even made irrelevant by AI. But I think the worse part of it, is how it has affected my ability to analize and do things.

## AI and - or the lack of - thinking

I think this is a point that has been beaten to exhaustion by now, but as someone that has lived AI adoption since the beginning, I can seriously attest to this happening to me and having to suffer and deal with it. AI is a dangerous tool. As much as it is powerfull, it hides a very dark truth, something I think is almost intrinsic to it's design: AI does not make you better at things, It makes you better at *delegating* things. I think if you are going to use AI, or regurlarly use AI, you need to recognize and be honest with yourself, that unless you are actively using certain strategies on it's usage, you are outsourcing your thinking, decision making, and analytics skills to AI. This should not be something so controversial, but surpringsingly it is, specially to the croud that "loves to make things" - well some of them, the mayority simply doesn't care.

When I see this brought up online, the most common answer I see is along the lines of 

- "Well, you need to use AI while also knowing what you are doing"
- "You have to use AI and also make it explain to you" 
- "You have to learn how to make the agent your teacher"

But I think this sentiments only try to justify the results of doing things with AI. I have it experienced myself first hand. At the beggining, we where only asking it to search things for us, and we made the decisions. Next, AI could made the research and also the decision making, you just had to make sure it didn't made a mistake, and have it explain itself. But, and I think this was the point of no return, now AI could do those things, without you having to worry about the accuracy. I think the moment we stopped questioning ourselves, we started to loose our intelligence. If you look at the world of AI development now, influencers, guides, tutorials, how current projects are run, we see an inversion of logic. We just need to have the AI do the work, and explain it as best as we can. If the AI made a mistake, well, you just didn't make a good job at explaining it. But, is that really the case? And more importantly, how could we explain? If we don't understand what we are explaining, and don't know how it works, then how can we be sure it's good? Of course, as long as the end result is good for the intented party, we can call it a day, but we no longer have control on what we built, and worse, we loose the abilities that make humans special.

And I might sound like a boomer, someone that is stuck on the past, and even though I agree, I also don't believe I'm wrong. I think the best example I can think of is the calculator and adjacent tecnologies. When the calculator came about, the jobs that required calculations had a change on how they did things. If an accountant for example had no idea how to use a calculator, they would fall behind, they will loose their job. A couple of years later, computers and programs like Excel appeared, and now you didn't even need to make the advance calculations, you just had to learn how to use the program. If you didn't know how to do it, you were out.

We can say this mimicks AI adoption in the same way. We need to adapt, and we also need to hone our skills. If a mathematician started to use a calculator, but didn't continue to study how integrals worked and only used the calculator functions, then without the tool, he will be useless. So "AI is not the problem", is what most will say, "You are, you need to study on your own, keep researching". And I would agree, but there is a catch with AI, and as corny as it sounds, I think is impossible to *actually* learn with AI. If you use AI to learn, you inevitably have to give things in exchange, like researching sources, verifying them, making decisions on what to use. And even if you take mind of each one of these things, you inevitably give up the most important part of learning, which is the conversation with yourself and what that entails. I think is important to learn by actually talking only to yourself, the human experience consists of learning by doing, not learning by telling someone to do something for me and then pretend I understood it on the first place - and YES, this does apply to the act of searching itself. Because only then you will realized how horribly has that skill made been worse without AI, and if you don't have that skill to being with, it will inevitably seep into your work with AI.

I think is hilarious, how I tool with intelligence in it's name, is incentivizing you to surrender yours. But that doesn't mean it has to be that way.

## How to commit, and feel good again.

As I had these worries the last couple of weeks, I realized I also had gave up a long time ago. As we need to eat and live, work became something I delegated. I was - and still am - just hired to read a ticket board, pass it to AI, tell it to do it, and mark it done. And if I wanted to improve behaviours or changes on this flow to avoid this, I was just quickly outpaced by my peers. To work on software, you can no longer afford to think about the software. You have to think how to best explain how the software has to be done. This was wreaking havoc on my mental health and my critical thinking skills without realizing it. I think what also is damaging to a developer is that we are currently in a veil where we pretend this is not the case, just to make us feel better. At work they tell us that we are using it as a tool, that we still need to verify the code, review it ourselves, not leave everything to AI. But the fact of the matter is that we leave things to AI in the end. No one reviews code any longer, not really, when I realized I could not longer understand the code I was looking at, all the while I was telling AI to review it and explain to made what it reviewed, and also make more code for me, is when I realized I had lost what I loved about software. And it took the same amount of time to find myself again, and realize what I needed to do to recover that passion and those abilities I though I had lost, or worse, never had in the first place.

If you think what I'm saying speaks to you, I will try to give to you the best advice I can. I think the answer is, if you love to do something, truly love it, and what that process means to you, then DON'T have AI do it for you. If you love to code, don't have AI make the code, take the time to code yourself, feel the struggle of learning things, of not knowing what you are doing most of the time, of making mistakes. If you love to write, don't have AI change your writing, or give you writing ideas. Have an idea first, even if it's terrible, make pacing mistakes, understand what could improve (for example, I could have this page go trough an editor, but I think it defeats the purpose of what I'm trying to do here). The same applies to video editing, drawing, electronics, cooking, sewing, building. One we love to do something, we put up with the parts people don't like to see or deal with. This is why I think people who are entering into coding just love doing "things". You get the dopamine rush of making something, without knowing how it works, and why it means to you.

So, as of late, I have decided that while I can't beat the AI wave at work and other parts of life, I am still on power to not let that affect me as much. I have decided for example, to leave this blog fully human, to learn my punctuation mistakes, to see how it reads afterwards. To code without AI on personal projects, to have to deal with code not working again and you don't know why, until you do. Because that is what makes us whole, is what makes us improve and enjoy our hobbies, not having our hobbies done for us.

Of course, this doesn't mean that outright rejecting AI is a good move. I still use AI sometimes to ask for sources on things, or to research latest approaches, or to give examples. But I know that I need to keep in mind that if I don't take my time from time to time to do things alone, my work with AI won't be good either. So what I have done, and you can do as well, is pick some topic you have interest in, and journal about it, without AI, by making the research yourself, looking at what to read on it, what not to read. Hell, even go to a library and dedicate an hour to it. Your brain will thank you, and your use of AI will also improve as well, trust me.

So at the end, my advice is this: AI is here to stay, but we are as well. Be intelligent, instead of having something else be it for you.
